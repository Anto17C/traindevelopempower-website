What Boards Should Ask Before Approving an AI Initiative

A fictional reporting proposal used to show the evidence directors can request before approving AI.

An AI proposal can promise faster reporting while leaving the board uncertain about what it is approving. A successful demonstration shows that a task can be performed. A decision to fund or expand its use also needs evidence about value, reliability, information access and responsibility when something goes wrong.

For proposals requiring board approval, directors can ask management to explain the intended business outcome and the conditions under which it can be achieved. The depth of review should reflect the significance of the decision and the organisation’s delegated authorities. Routine uses can remain within management’s approved policies and limits.

Start with the decision being requested

Consider a fictional company whose management wants to introduce AI-assisted monthly performance reporting across its business units. A demonstration produces a readable commentary quickly, and the proposal asks the board to approve a companywide rollout.

The demonstration uses selected reports. It does not measure the time spent checking the output, test difficult reporting periods or explain whether confidential forecasts will be sent to an external service. The proposal also leaves unclear who would approve each report.

These gaps do not establish that the initiative should be rejected. They identify the evidence needed to judge whether a broader rollout is justified. A useful first question is what approval would permit: a limited trial, a financial commitment, access to sensitive information or a change to the way decisions are made.

Test the business case against the complete workflow

Ask management to describe the existing process and the improvement it expects. For the fictional reporting proposal, the relevant comparison includes preparing the source data, producing a draft, checking calculations, investigating exceptions and approving the final report.

A faster first draft may leave total preparation time unchanged if reviewers spend longer finding and correcting errors. Conversely, better analysis or more consistent reporting may justify investment even when time savings are modest. The business case should explain which outcome matters and how it will be measured.

Request the full costs, including software, integration, training, review and ongoing support. Distinguish staff time released for other work from a cash saving. State the assumptions behind expected benefits and the conditions under which the initiative would no longer be worthwhile.

Establish what the system can access and do

The same AI model can sit inside very different processes. An assistant that drafts commentary from an approved file has different permissions from a connected system that retrieves live records, sends reports or updates business systems.

For the reporting proposal, ask which data sources are available, where information is processed, who can access it and how retention is controlled. Establish whether the system only drafts text or can also take actions. Any move from drafting to sending or changing records deserves an explicit review of authority and controls.

Management should obtain the relevant security, privacy and legal assessments for the proposed use. The board needs a clear account of material findings and unresolved issues, supported by people qualified to assess them. A supplier’s general description of its product does not establish that the company’s configuration and use are appropriate.

Request evidence that reflects real operating conditions

Testing should cover the work the system will actually perform. For monthly reporting, that includes unusual cost movements, incomplete inputs, conflicting source documents and periods when performance deteriorates. Selected examples of fluent commentary provide little evidence about those conditions. For a worked numerical example of checking an AI-written commentary against source figures, see How to Check AI-Generated Financial Analysis.

The following questions turn the fictional proposal into a more useful decision paper.

Illustrative questions and evidence for the fictional reporting proposal
Question for managementEvidence to request
Does the process improve reporting?Comparison with the current process, including review time, correction effort and agreed quality measures.
Can reviewers detect significant errors?Test results showing factual mistakes, unsupported explanations and whether reviewers caught them before approval.
Are information and permissions controlled?Documented data access, handling arrangements and limits on actions the system can take.
Who can approve use and intervene?Named business owner, approval responsibilities, escalation route and a workable fallback process.
What would justify expansion?Agreed success measures, unresolved issues, limits of the trial and the decision required before wider use.

These are illustrative questions for this reporting case. Different uses require different evidence. A customer-facing credit decision, for example, raises additional questions about affected customers and decision rights that a draft internal commentary does not resolve.

NIST’s voluntary AI Risk Management Framework provides a broader reference for evaluating and managing AI risks across the system lifecycle. It can inform the review without replacing the organisation’s own obligations or decision process. NIST AI Risk Management Framework

Make accountability and assurance explicit

A statement that a human will review every output needs practical detail. Identify who reviews it, what they check, what information they use and whether they have enough time and authority to stop circulation. A reviewer who cannot inspect the underlying figures cannot reliably validate a financial commentary.

For the fictional company, the finance function could own the reporting process, with designated managers approving final reports. Relevant control functions could challenge the design and test evidence. Internal audit’s role would depend on its mandate and risk assessment; its independent assurance should remain distinct from running the process.

The OECD’s accountability principle calls for responsibility appropriate to each actor’s role, traceability and continuing risk management. That supports asking for a record of the evidence, decisions and corrective actions behind an AI deployment. OECD AI principle on accountability

Set the conditions for proceeding and returning to the board

In this example, a proportionate decision could permit a limited trial in selected business units using approved information, with every report checked before circulation. Management would return with measured benefits, error findings, review costs and outstanding risks before requesting wider use.

The decision record should state the scope, budget and owner, along with success measures and conditions for pausing the trial. A significant error, unauthorised disclosure or failure of a required control could trigger escalation. The responsible teams should agree thresholds appropriate to the use before the trial begins.

Oversight continues after approval. Changes to the supplier, model, data sources or permitted actions may affect earlier conclusions. Management reporting should show whether benefits persist, what incidents or exceptions occurred and whether the agreed controls still work.
