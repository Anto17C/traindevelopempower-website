How to Evaluate a Petroleum Project: NPV, IRR and Risk Analysis

A worked, illustrative example of how to test whether an oil development clears its investment hurdle and how much room it has.

A petroleum project can clear its investment hurdle at US$70 per barrel and fall short at US$65. To find out how much room the project has, build a cash-flow forecast, calculate net present value (NPV) and internal rate of return (IRR), then test which assumptions can change the decision. The worked example below uses NPV and IRR for a simplified oil development. Other evaluation tools include expected monetary value (EMV), profitability index (PI) and payback period.

1. Build the project cash-flow forecast

Start with saleable production by period and the price the project expects to receive at the point of sale. Model the production ramp-up and decline, and check when development spending occurs relative to first production (first oil or first gas). From sales revenue, deduct operating and capital expenditure and include the cash cost of decommissioning in the periods when it is expected to occur.

Define whose cash flow you are valuing. For an investor appraisal, apply the terms of the relevant licence or contract and the applicable fiscal regime. Royalties, taxes and production-sharing terms can change both the amount and timing of cash available to the investor. State whether the model is pre- or post-tax and before or after financing. Match the discount rate to that cash-flow basis and use consistent assumptions about currency and inflation.

Discounted cash flow (DCF) accounts for the time value of money by expressing future cash flows in present-value terms. Timing matters over the long lives of petroleum projects, including when the forecast uses constant dollars.

2. Calculate NPV and IRR

NPV is the sum of the present values of all project cash flows, including the initial investment. For annual cash flows, divide the cash flow in year t by (1 + r) raised to the power t, where r is the annual discount rate. Cash flow at year zero enters at face value. A positive NPV means the forecast cash flows exceed the required return in present-value terms. On a consistent appraisal basis, a higher NPV represents more estimated value.

IRR is the discount rate at which NPV equals zero. With a single initial outflow followed by positive cash flows, compare IRR with the required return. Report NPV alongside IRR so the decision-maker can see the amount of value at stake. Late negative cash flows, such as a standalone decommissioning outflow, can create multiple IRRs or leave no meaningful IRR. In those cases, focus on NPV at the required return and inspect the cash-flow profile. A higher IRR indicates a higher percentage return for conventional cash flows, but does not necessarily identify the project that creates the most value. When mutually exclusive projects rank differently under NPV and IRR, use NPV as the primary value criterion on a consistent appraisal basis.

3. Work through an illustrative oil development

Assume a hypothetical development costs US$100 million at year zero and sells 1 million barrels per year for five years. The realised price is US$70 per barrel and cash operating costs are US$40 per barrel. Decommissioning costs US$10 million at the end of year five. Use a 10% annual discount rate and year-end cash flows.

The example uses constant dollars and a 10% real discount rate for illustration. It assumes flat production and excludes fiscal payments, financing and working capital. The inputs are invented and represent neither a field forecast nor an industry benchmark.

Annual revenue is US$70 million. After US$40 million in operating costs, annual cash flow is US$30 million in years one to four and US$20 million in year five after decommissioning. Discount those five cash flows at 10% and subtract the year-zero investment. The result is US$7.51 million NPV, with an IRR of 13.04%.

Holding all other base-case assumptions fixed, the price at which NPV equals zero is about US$68.02/bbl. That leaves around US$1.98/bbl between the assumed base price and the project’s economic threshold in this model.

4. Use the downside to decide what to investigate

Test oil price and development cost individually to see which changes can move the project below its hurdle. Then combine assumptions that could move together. A delayed start, for example, could push receipts into later periods while increasing development costs. A combined downside shows the effect of those changes on value and helps the team decide which assumptions need stronger evidence.

The base case in this example clears the 10% hurdle, but either downside alone brings NPV below zero. Before recommending approval, an appraisal team could test the realised-price assumptions, validate the development cost estimate, and evaluate whether phasing the investment or changing its scope would improve the downside. For a real project, rerun the analysis with the applicable fiscal terms and check how production uncertainty, schedule and abandonment costs affect the result.
